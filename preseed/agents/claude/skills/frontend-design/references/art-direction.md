# Art direction

Read this when establishing or materially changing frontend visual direction.

## Derive identity from the product

Build from evidence specific to the subject:

- product behavior, data, tools, vocabulary, environment, and physical metaphors;
- audience culture, attention level, working conditions, and expectations;
- brand history, existing identity, content voice, and real assets;
- the action or understanding the surface must produce;
- trust, energy, intimacy, authority, urgency, or calm the experience must earn.

Originality is not random novelty. A signature should reveal something true about the product. If removing the logo also removes the product identity, the composition is decoration around a brand mark.

## Create meaningful alternatives

For greenfield work or substantial redesign with unresolved direction, develop two or three complete visual languages. Each direction should define:

1. **Concept:** the product truth that drives the form.
2. **Composition:** dominant geometry, reading order, density, and first viewport.
3. **Hierarchy:** what leads, supports, and stays quiet.
4. **Typography:** display, reading, utility, and data roles.
5. **Palette logic:** semantic roles, contrast, and distribution, not a list of fashionable hex values.
6. **Material:** flat, tactile, editorial, technical, photographic, illustrative, spatial, or another justified surface language.
7. **Imagery:** subject, framing, treatment, provenance, and failure behavior.
8. **Interaction:** tempo, feedback, transitions, and reduced-motion form.
9. **Signature:** one recognizable product-derived element.
10. **Risk:** the honest cost or failure mode.

Alternatives must differ across most of these dimensions. Three accent colors on the same hero and card grid are one direction pretending to be three.

When direction is delegated, compare alternatives internally, choose the strongest fit, state the choice and assumptions, and continue.

## Build a coherent thesis

Return to the core direction contract after selection. Intensity belongs where it advances hierarchy or signature; restraint protects everything around that moment.

Use references to extract abstract qualities: composition, rhythm, density, contrast, typography, material, image treatment, interaction tempo, and emotional tone. Do not clone layouts, branding, illustrations, or proprietary components. Name what was learned, not what will be copied.

## Diagnose generic output

Treat these as warning signs, not universal bans:

- centered hero followed by interchangeable feature cards;
- text-left and decorative-blob-right composition;
- unmotivated gradients or glass surfaces;
- decorative bento grids and excessive pills;
- huge type unsupported by the rest of the composition;
- one card treatment for unrelated content;
- purple-on-black or stock dashboard defaults;
- random animation, rounded rectangles everywhere, and placeholder copy;
- invented metrics, testimonials, portraits, or proof;
- the same palette, hero, motif, and rhythm across unrelated products.

Any pattern can be right when product, content, and concept justify it. Fix the reasoning or composition behind a failure rather than adding a permanent prohibition.

## Test specificity

Ask:

- Could a competitor use this unchanged?
- Does the first viewport show what only this product can do or mean?
- Is the hierarchy derived from the user's job?
- Does the signature survive without effects?
- Do typography, color, material, imagery, and motion describe the same world?
- What is intentionally quiet?

If another company can be substituted without friction, return to product evidence before implementation.
